We evaluate on \MSSections{} sections spanning four technologies
(\MSRuns{} runs), with the section as the unit of analysis: every model sees
identical held-out blocks, so a paired test removes the between-section variance
that otherwise dominates. NRDO attains Pearson $r$ \MSNMOPearson{} against
\MSBestBaselinePearson{} for the closest baseline (\MSBestBaseline{}).

All \MSNBaselines{} baselines are beaten with Holm-corrected
$p \le \MSMaxHolm$ (Appendix~\ref{app:empirical}), and the \emph{smallest} effect size
across the family is $d_z = \MSMinDz$, which is large by conventional
standards; NRDO wins on at least \MSMinWins{} of \MSNPairs{} sections against
every comparator. The advantage is therefore consistent across anatomies rather
than an artifact of one tissue: the twelve MERFISH sections span the
anterior--posterior axis of the same brain with assay, panel and preprocessing
held fixed, so the variation between them is anatomical.

\paragraph{The gain comes from continuity, not from message passing.}
Two controls localize the effect, both measured on this benchmark. A non-spatial
autoencoder sees no coordinates and reaches \MSAEPearson{} purely through the
inverse-distance read-out head shared by every graph baseline, so the gap between
it and a graph model isolates \emph{message passing}: across \MSNGraphModels{}
graph models that margin spans \MSWorstGraphOverAE{} to \MSBestGraphOverAE{}
Pearson $r$, against \MSNMOOverAE{} for NRDO --- an order of magnitude larger. An
implicit neural field, continuous by construction but carrying no dynamics, is
the weakest model tested (\MSWeakestPearson{}, a gap of $\MSMaxDelta$). Continuity
alone is therefore insufficient and message passing adds little over a good
read-out head; it is the \emph{operator} that carries the gain, which the
parameter-matched $T{=}0$ control confirms (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}).

\paragraph{Spatial autocorrelation.}
NRDO leads on SSIM (\MSNMOSSIM{} against \MSBestAnySSIM{} for the best baseline,
\MSBestAnySSIMModel{}) but has by some margin the largest error in Moran's $I$
(\MSNMOMoran{} against \MSBestAnyMoran{} for \MSBestAnyMoranModel{}, a deficit of
\MSMoranDelta{}): the predicted fields are markedly more spatially autocorrelated
than the measurements. On the primary Visium section, where the diagnostic can be
read directly, the predicted map has $I = \NMOMoranPred$ against \MoranTrue{}
measured. SSIM measures local contrast on a rasterized map and Moran's $I$
global autocorrelation on the point set, and the pattern is the expected
signature of a dissipative operator attenuating high spatial frequencies --- the
same diffusion-driven smoothing analyzed for graph neural diffusion
\citep{grand2021,gread2022}. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} discusses the remedy.

\subsection{The numerical machinery is load-bearing}
\label{sec:numerics}

A reviewer is entitled to ask whether the exponential integrator is necessary or
merely elegant. Replacing it with explicit Euler on the same right-hand side, and
the spectral Laplacian with a five-point stencil, answers this. The exponential
scheme was stable at every step size tested, up to $\Delta t = \NumStableDt$, or
$\NumCFLRatio\times$ the CFL bound; since it never destabilized, that ratio is a
lower bound imposed by the sweep grid rather than a measured threshold, which is
what Proposition~\ref{prop:contraction} predicts. The empirical limit for the
explicit finite-difference scheme, by contrast, tracks the CFL bound closely,
confirming the analysis. To integrate a fixed horizon to a $10^{-2}$ relative
tolerance the exponential scheme needs \NumStrangSteps{} steps against
\NumEulerSteps{} for explicit Euler, a $\NumSpeedup\times$ difference in cost
(Appendix~\ref{app:empirical}; the step grid is coarse and Euler overshoots the
tolerance, so this is an upper bound on the ratio at that accuracy).
The fair statement is therefore not that the alternatives fail but that they are
an order of magnitude more expensive for the same answer, while additionally
imposing a step-size restriction that couples to \emph{learned} parameters and
can be violated mid-training as $\mD_\theta$ adapts.

\subsection{The reconstruction does not degrade tissue-level structure}
\label{sec:biology}

Correlation does not establish that a reconstruction remains usable for the
analyses a biologist would run on it, so we cluster the \emph{predicted}
expression at held-out locations and score it against annotations shipped with
the public data, over \BioSections{} sections with reference labels
(Appendix~\ref{app:empirical}, Table~\ref{tab:biology}). NRDO retains
\BioARIRet{} of the adjusted Rand index obtainable from the measured data against
\BioARIRetBest{} for the best baseline, and is nominally ahead on marker AUROC
and $k$-NN neighborhood preservation.

We do not read this as a second win. Each margin is a small fraction of its own
dispersion --- $\BioARIRetGapSD$, $\BioMarkerGapSD$ and $\BioKNNGapSD$ pooled
standard deviations --- over too few annotated sections to support a paired test,
and we report none. The defensible claim is the negative one: the operator
attains its reconstruction accuracy \emph{without} degrading the tissue-level
structure a downstream analysis depends on, where a model that smoothed its way
to a good correlation would have lost it. The reference labels are themselves an
algorithm's output rather than independent ground truth.

\subsection{Learned dynamics}


\paragraph{The recovered length scale is close to its own initialization.}
The fitted operator has a median diffusion length of \NullBaseline\,$\mu$m on the
primary section. An earlier version of this work noted that this is the same
order as measured morphogen gradients \citep{kicheva2007kinetics,yu2009fgf8}; a
control retracts that observation. Refitting the identical architecture at the
identical budget on \emph{coordinate-shuffled} sections --- expression permuted
across positions, preserving every marginal and destroying all spatial structure
--- recovers \NullShuffled\,$\mu$m at $r = \NullShuffledR$, against
\NullInit\,$\mu$m untrained: a move of \NullShuffledShift\% from initialization
against \NullDataShift\% for real tissue. Sweeping the splat bandwidth and the
lattice resolution leaves the value unchanged in microns while it moves by a
factor of four in lattice cells, so neither is the mechanism
(Table~\ref{tab:difflen_null}). We therefore withdraw the correspondence with
measured gradient lengths: the median is largely a configuration constant. What
the data do determine is the \emph{spread} --- at initialization every channel
carries an identical length scale, and training differentiates them across
\DiffLenLo--\DiffLenHi\,$\mu$m. The anisotropy is
used rather than merely available: constraining $\mD$ to be isotropic costs
\AblIsotropicDelta{} Pearson $r$.

The dispersion relation (Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamics}c) decreases monotonically in
$|\vk|$, so the fitted operator is dissipative and supports no Turing band. This
is what the training objective selects for: \eqref{eq:steady} rewards making the
\emph{observed} configuration stationary, whereas a Turing-unstable operator
would render the homogeneous state unstable so that structure grows
spontaneously. Recovering a pattern-forming regime would require observations
away from steady state, in line with Theorem~\ref{thm:identifiability}.

\subsection{Transfer across tissue, species and resolution}

Transfer is the weakest setting here, and we report it under the same
held-out-block protocol as everything else. An earlier version conditioned the
model on a random half of the target and scored the complement, which is not
comparable: on the Xenium target the median distance from a scored location to
the nearest visible one is \SplitXenRandom\,$\mu$m under a random split against
\SplitXenBlock\,$\mu$m under blocks, so the random split admits exactly the
neighbor leakage that motivated blocks in the first place. Under that protocol a
zero-shot model appeared to beat models trained on the target; under blocks it
does not, and the numbers below are the corrected ones.

Applied unchanged from adult mouse brain to human breast carcinoma, NRDO reaches
\TissueZeroShot{} against a training-mean floor of \TissueFloor{} and an
in-domain oracle of \TissueOracle{} --- that is, essentially no transfer.
Cross-resolution is better but still poor: an operator fitted to 55\,$\mu$m
Visium spots and evaluated on single-cell Xenium of the same organ reaches
\ResZeroShot{} over \ResSharedGenes{} shared genes against an oracle of
\ResOracle{}. The \TissueZeroShotBaselineName{} baseline transfers better in both
settings. We attribute this to the mechanism that produces the in-domain gain:
NRDO fits an operator whose fixed point is the source tissue, and relaxation
toward that attractor is inappropriate when the target has a different
architecture, whereas local message passing encodes less about the source.
Proposition~\ref{prop:nystrom} makes the distinction precise --- discretization
invariance of the \emph{encoder} does not entail portability of the \emph{fitted}
operator. Until $\mD_\theta$ is conditioned on tissue-level covariates the
operator should be regarded as a within-section model. Full tables and the
developmental-forecasting result are in Appendix~\ref{app:empirical}.

\subsection{Counterfactual perturbation}
\label{sec:results4}

We test whether the latent field encodes pathway-specific structure with a
split-half design: inject the latent direction that maximally raises one random
half of a morphogen pathway, integrate the operator, and ask whether the
\emph{held-out} half responds preferentially against a permutation null over
matched gene sets. Across \BeadNPathways{} pathways the enrichment does not reach
significance (\BeadNSig{} significant; mean held-out rank \BeadMeanRank\% against
a 50\% chance level).

Theorem~\ref{thm:identifiability} predicts this: the perturbation displaces
$\vz$ off the observed manifold $\mathcal{R} = \vz^\star(\Omega)$, where a single
stationary snapshot places no constraint on $f_\theta$. We therefore read the
latent--pathway correlations as coarse anatomy that pathway scores also track,
not as a pathway-specific representation. The experiment does establish a spatial
scale --- the response decays to half its peak within \BeadWidth\,$\mu$m. A
second pre-specified probe against Perturb-seq \citep{norman2019perturbseq} was
underpowered ($n = \PSNPerturb$) and is inconclusive
(Appendix~\ref{app:empirical}).

\paragraph{Can the smoothness be trained away?}
The obvious remedy is a loss term matching the radially averaged power spectrum
of the predicted field to that of the measured field. We implement it and sweep
its weight in two forms (Appendix~\ref{app:empirical},
Table~\ref{tab:spectral}). The result is a bound rather than a fix: the better
form buys $\SpecShapePctMoran\%$ of $|\Delta I|$ for $\SpecShapePctR\%$ of the
Pearson $r$ at its optimum, but across every weight and both forms the predicted
autocorrelation bottoms out at $I_{\mathrm{pred}} = \SpecMinIPred$ against a
measured $\SpecITrue$. Weighting the term heavily enough to destroy predictive
accuracy still does not bring the reconstruction near the roughness of the data,
so the smoothness belongs to the operator class rather than to the objective. We
keep $\lambda = 0$ throughout.

\paragraph{Robustness to sampling density.}
Spatial transcriptomics is noisy and sampled at whatever density the assay
provides, so we re-train every model under \RobAxes{} corruption axes applied
identically to all of them (\RobRuns{} runs; Appendix~\ref{app:empirical},
Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). On two axes NRDO is not the most robust: under
multiplicative count noise every model retains about the same fraction
(\RobNoiseNMO\% against \RobNoiseBest\% for \RobNoiseBestModel{}), and under
random location dropout the graph baselines degrade slightly less. The exception
is the axis the continuous formulation actually predicts. Subsampling the section
to an eighth of its locations --- changing sampling density without changing
anatomy --- NRDO retains \RobDensNMO\% of its full-density accuracy against
\RobDensBest\% for the best baseline. A model defined on a field rather than on a
lattice should survive exactly this perturbation, and it is the one setting where
it clearly does.

\paragraph{Is the benchmark's ordering the converged ordering?}
Not exactly, and the direction favors us. Training every model to a shared
early-stopping criterion on the full \ConvSection{} section --- the one whose
ordering changes with budget --- NRDO attains \ConvNMO{} against \ConvBest{} for
the strongest baseline, winning \ConvWins{}/\ConvNPairs{} seeds with
$d_z = \ConvMinDz$--$\ConvMaxDz$, having \emph{lost} to it at the benchmark
budget (Appendix~\ref{app:empirical}, Table~\ref{tab:converged}). Two findings
survive convergence: the non-spatial autoencoder still matches the graph models,
so their near-tie is a property of the task rather than of undertraining; and
NRDO retains the largest error in Moran's $I$, so the over-smoothing is
structural.

\subsection{Further results}
The exponential integrator costs
$\NumSpeedup\times$ fewer steps than explicit Euler to reach the same
tolerance; the reconstruction does not degrade spatial-domain structure,
marker localization or $k$-NN neighborhoods; zero-shot transfer retains part
of the learned structure but is beaten by the STAGATE-style baseline; and a
counterfactual perturbation probe returns a null result that
Theorem~\ref{thm:identifiability} predicts in advance. All are reported in
full in the archival version.

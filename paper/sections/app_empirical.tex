\subsection{Spectral matching}
\subsubsection*{Full discussion}
\label{sec:spectral}


Section~\ref{sec:results} reports that our predicted fields are too smooth, and
Section~\ref{sec:convergence} shows this is structural rather than an artifact of
the training budget. The natural remedy is a term matching the radially averaged
power spectrum of the predicted field to that of the measured field. We
implement it, sweep its weight, and report the trade-off
(Table~\ref{tab:spectral}, Fig.~\ref{fig:spectral}; \SpecSeeds{} seeds per
point).

The form of the term matters more than its weight. Matching the \emph{absolute}
spectrum over all bands leaves an amplitude degree of freedom the model can
exploit without redistributing energy across scales --- a controlled check finds
it penalizes a purely rescaled field more heavily than a genuinely smoothed one
--- and its best setting costs $\SpecFullPctR\%$ of the Pearson $r$ to gain
$\SpecFullPctMoran\%$ of $|\Delta I|$. Matching the spectrum \emph{normalized}
by total power over the upper half of the band removes that freedom and
penalizes the frequencies a dissipative operator actually loses. At
$\lambda = \SpecShapeBestW$ it reaches $|\Delta I| = \SpecShapeBestMoran$,
better than the absolute form achieves at any weight, for $\SpecShapePctR\%$ of
the accuracy; the curve turns over above that, so it is an optimum rather than a
point on a slope.

The conclusion is a bound, not a fix. Across every weight and both forms the
predicted autocorrelation bottoms out at $I_{\mathrm{pred}} = \SpecMinIPred$
against a measured $\SpecITrue$: weighting the term heavily enough to destroy
predictive accuracy still does not bring the reconstruction near the roughness of
the data. We keep $\lambda = 0$ elsewhere and conclude that the smoothness
belongs to the operator class rather than to the objective.

\subsection{Converged single-section comparison}
\label{sec:convergence}
\subsubsection*{Full discussion}

The benchmark trains every model for a fixed, reduced number of epochs, so its
ordering could be a fact about the budget rather than the models. We settle this
on \ConvSection{}, the section where the ordering changes with budget, training
every model to a shared early-stopping criterion on the full section with
\ConvSeeds{} seeds (Appendix~\ref{app:empirical}, Table~\ref{tab:converged}).
NRDO attains \ConvNMO{} against \ConvBest{} for the strongest baseline
(\ConvBestModel), winning on \ConvWins{}/\ConvNPairs{} seeds against every
comparator with $d_z = \ConvMinDz$--$\ConvMaxDz$, having \emph{lost} to that
baseline at the benchmark budget. The budget is therefore conservative for NRDO
here rather than neutral: Table~\ref{tab:multisection} establishes consistency
across anatomy at a fixed budget, not that its margins are the converged margins.

Two findings survive convergence and matter more than the ordering. The
non-spatial autoencoder still matches the graph models (the best leads it by
\ConvGraphOverAE{} Pearson $r$), so their near-tie is a property of this task
rather than of undertraining. And NRDO retains the largest error in Moran's $I$
(\ConvNMOMoran{} against \ConvBestMoran{}), so the over-smoothing is structural.
NRDO costs \ConvNMOWall\,s against \ConvBaseWallLo--\ConvBaseWallHi\,s per run on
one CPU.
